3. Three labels, plus permission to pass

What does “training” do?

The model reads each circle’s red, green, and blue amounts (RGB). Training calculates an average RGB color for each label. A new circle is compared with all three averages; the closest average wins, unless all three are too far away. Then the page says Unknown / no good match.

The three average colors will appear here after training.

Beyond “Dark” vs. “Light”

Dark vs. Light has two possible labels: a binary classifier. This model has three: a multiclass classifier. The RGB input has three numbers, and here we also chose three output labels. That is a coincidence: the same RGB inputs could be used for two labels or many more. A pale red and a dark red can both have the label Red.

Try Black or White. These colors are too far from all three learned averages, so the page says Unknown / no good match. Unknown is a distance rule, not a fourth trained label. Some unfamiliar colors, such as gray or purple, may still be close enough to get a label. This rule does not catch every bad guess!

A peek inside the score

This is a nearest-centroid classifier. Training learns three average RGB vectors. For prediction, RGB values are divided by 255, and we calculate squared distance to each average. Each match strength is exp(−distance / 0.15); dividing by the sum gives the three scores. The value 0.15 controls how sharp the scores look and is fixed for this demo.

The Unknown rule uses ordinary RGB distance after dividing each channel by 255. If the nearest average is more than 0.70 away, the model passes. This fixed teaching cutoff keeps all nine training examples and rejects black and white. It is not a validated accuracy guarantee.

We have not measured accuracy on a separate test set. Trying the training colors again checks that the demo works; it does not prove that the model understands every shade.
